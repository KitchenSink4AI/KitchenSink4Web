What I learned about pricing a small hardware product

I spent most of last spring trying to work out what to charge for a thing I had already built, which is the wrong order to do it in and I would not recommend it to anyone. The number I landed on was $49.99, and I want to write down how I got there while I still remember the reasoning, because three months from now it will look obvious and it was not obvious at all.

Pricing

The first thing anybody tells you is to look at what comparable products charge, which is good advice right up until you notice that the comparable products are not actually comparable and that their manufacturers have a supply chain you do not have. I looked at eleven of them anyway. The cheapest sat under twenty dollars and felt like it, and the most expensive was north of two hundred and came with a service contract nobody wanted.

What about the price?

People kept asking me that in exactly those words, usually before they had asked what the product does, and I came to think of it as a signal rather than a question. A buyer who leads with price has already decided the thing is a commodity, and no amount of clever positioning changes a mind that is already made up about which shelf you belong on.

Reviews

Nine months in the reviews average 3.8 out of 5, across roughly 400 reviews, which I am told is respectable and which still stings every time I read the one-star ones. The complaints cluster: the manual is thin, the packaging is over-engineered, and the thing is heavier than the photographs suggest. All three are true.

The honest summary is that I charged what I needed to charge and then spent a year finding out whether anyone agreed with me.

If I were starting again I would price it before I built it, or at least before I ordered tooling, and I would write the number on a wall where I had to look at it every day.
