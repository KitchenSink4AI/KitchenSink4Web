The AI Tools Every College Student Uses (And What They Won’t Tell You)

Utkarsh Patil
4 min read
·
Just now

--

Everyone in my group chat has a favorite AI tool, and none of them agree. One friend swears by Claude for essays. Another won’t touch anything but ChatGPT. A guy in my mechanical design class treats Claude Code like a second teammate. Every time someone drops a new link in the chat, I try it, use it twice, and then it either sticks or quietly dies in my bookmarks.

After doing this enough, I’ve noticed something the “top 10 AI tools” posts skip: each tool has a specific way it lets you down. Not in a dramatic crash-and-burn way. In the small, annoying way you only notice once you’re already depending on it.

So here’s the version with the parts the marketing pages leave out.

For writing

ChatGPT is what most people open first because it does a bit of everything. That breadth is also the problem when you care about the output. Ask it for an essay and you’ll get something competent, structured, and weirdly flat. It has a voice, and that voice is not yours. You have to fight it to sound like a person.

Claude holds instructions better across a long document, which matters if you’re editing section by section instead of writing in one sitting. But it hedges more than I’d like. It’ll circle around a direct answer when a blunter tool would just say it.

Grammarly is fine for catching the typo you missed at 2am. It won’t help you figure out why your argument doesn’t hold together. If you let it rewrite too many sentences, your essay starts sounding like it was edited by HR.

QuillBot rewrites a clunky sentence fast. It also has a tell: over-smoothed, grammatically perfect, completely lifeless. Professors have gotten good at spotting that.

For research

Perplexity is the fastest way to fact-check something at midnight because it shows where the answer came from instead of just asserting it. But it’s built for speed, not depth. For anything beyond a quick check, it starts to feel thin.

NotebookLM only pulls from what you upload, which is reassuring when you’re studying from specific readings and don’t want it inventing things. The tradeoff is obvious: it’s only as smart as what you fed it. Forget to upload the right chapter and it just won’t know.

For coding

Copilot lives inside your editor and gives you decent autocomplete, which is most of what people actually want. It won’t reason through your whole codebase. It finishes lines; it doesn’t understand your project.

Claude Code can run for a while on its own, digging through a messy repo and actually fixing things, which is impressive the first time you see it. It also lives in the terminal, which is a real wall if you’re not comfortable there yet. It’s easy to burn through usage faster than you expect if you leave it running.

Replit gets you from an idea to something running online with almost no setup — great for a class project due in six hours. Don’t expect to build anything serious on it long-term. You’ll outgrow it fast.

For notes and planning

Notion AI is convenient mostly because it’s already inside the app you use for everything else. On its own, as a thinking tool, it’s kind of mediocre. It’s a feature bolted onto a workspace, not something you’d choose in isolation.

Otter.ai is the one tool on this list doing a job chatbots genuinely can’t: transcribing a lecture with speaker labels while it happens. It falls apart the second there’s crosstalk or a bad mic, and you’ll still spend twenty minutes afterward cleaning it up.

What I’ve actually taken away from all this

None of these tools are competing with each other as much as it looks from the outside. They’re solving different, narrow problems, and the moment you expect one to do another’s job, it lets you down. Free tiers work fine right up until you’re relying on the tool daily — then the caps show up and you’re stuck deciding if $20 a month is worth it for something that mostly just removes a limit.

I don’t think there’s a “best” AI tool for college. There’s just the one that happens to be doing your specific job right now, and the four others sitting quietly in your bookmarks for when it isn’t.

What’s your most-used AI tool this semester, and what’s the one thing about it that quietly annoys you?
